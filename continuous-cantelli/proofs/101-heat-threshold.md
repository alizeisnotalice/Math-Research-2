# The extinction curve for the full family of Gaussian windows

Let h=f−1 be a bounded, continuous, nonconstant exact normalized graph, P_w the Gaussian convolution with variance w, and B=sup h>0. Set J(w,x,b)=P_w[(exp(−bh)−1)/b](x), using the smooth value at b=0. At w=1, round 85 gives the exact strict signs: for b>0, J is positive on (−√(2b),√(2b)), negative outside its closure. Pointwise J is strictly increasing in real b because the divided difference of exp(−bh) is strictly increasing at each h≠0.

For every w>1 there exists a unique finite critical b=β(w)>0. Below it J is strictly negative at every center. At it J is zero at exactly one center ξ(w), strictly negative elsewhere, and its spatial second derivative at the contact is strictly negative. Above it some center gives a strictly positive value. Both β and ξ are real analytic on (1,∞), and

    β'(w)=−J_xx/(2J_b)>0 at (w,ξ(w),β(w)).
    β(w) ≥ log(1+B(w−1)/w)/(2B).

**Uniform lower bound.** Write K_a(y)=(exp(−ay)−1)/a=−y∫₀¹exp(−auy)du. Convolve the identity P₁[K_{t²/2}(h)](t)=0 with p_{w−1}(x−t)dt. After conditioning on the argument y of h, the intermediate location t is normal with mean (x+(w−1)y)/w and variance v=(w−1)/w. Thus J is the average against p_w(x−y)dy of K_b(h(y))−E K_{t²/2}(h(y)). The zero identity is integrable: its positive and negative parts at each t are equal and its negative part is bounded by B. Since K_a is increasing in a, the smallest conditional average occurs for zero normal mean, by the interval probabilities of an ordinary normal. For a centered normal t, E exp(−uht²/2)=(1+vuh)^(−1/2); h≥−1 ensures integrability. For 0<b≤log(1+Bv)/(2B), the decreasing function log(1+vz)/z on z>−1/v gives exp(−buh)≥(1+vuh)^(−1/2) for h>0 and the reverse for h<0. Multiplication by −h yields K_b≤E K_{t²/2}, strictly for nonzero h on integrating over u. This proves strict negativity throughout this range. At sufficiently large b the negative h on an open interval gives J(w,0,b)>0.

**Compactness of possible contacts.** For b in a fixed compact positive range the initial profile j_b(t)=J(1,t,b) has its positive part in a common compact interval. On two fixed intervals strictly to the left and right it is uniformly negative. In p_{w−1}*j_b divide by p_{w−1}(x); the sign of the resulting bilateral Laplace integral is negative at sufficiently large |x| by comparing the central positive mass with the farther fixed negative interval. This is uniform for w in compact subsets of (1,∞). Thus the supremum of b for which J≤0 everywhere is attained with at least one finite contact. Strict monotonicity in b gives the signs on both sides.

**Uniqueness and nondegeneracy.** At a contact x₀ the signed measure dμ=j_b(t)p_{w−1}(x₀−t)dt has zero mass and first moment. It has strictly positive density between ±√(2b) and strictly negative density on the two exterior intervals. Therefore ∫t² dμ=∫(t²−2b)dμ<0, giving J_xx<0. For any c≠0 subtract from exp(ct) the line interpolating its values at ±√(2b); the difference is negative in the central interval and positive outside. Its μ integral is strictly negative. The Gaussian shift formula makes J negative at every x≠x₀. The equations J=J_x=0 now have invertible Jacobian in b,x because J_b>0. Gaussian convolution and the bounded mark make J real analytic locally for w>0, by complex domination. The analytic branch given by the implicit function theorem continues to be the critical contact by the same signed-measure argument. The heat equation gives the displayed derivative.

**Entrance at w=1.** Put d=w−1 and define K(x,b)=J(1,x,b)/(b−x²/2) with its removable analytic values. This factorization follows by integrating J_b along the segment from x²/2 to b. K(0,0)=Eγ h²/2>0. Write K, K_x, K_xx without arguments for their values at (0,0). There is an analytic solution to J=J_x=0 near (w,x,b)=(1,0,0), since J_b=K and J_xx=−K there. The heat equation and the Taylor series of (b−x²/2)K(x,b) yield

    ξ(1+d)=−(K_x/K)d+O(d²),
    β(1+d)=d/2 + [(log K(·,0))″(0)/2] d²+O(d³).

Its b is positive for small d>0; the signed-measure argument identifies it with the global curve. In terms of U=P₁h, K(x,0)=2U(x)/x² by continuity. Thus the leading slope is universal, and the next coefficient is determined by three translated Gaussian derivatives U″, U‴, U⁽⁴⁾ at zero. These results are necessary conditions for exact graphs and also apply to bounded randomized marks with the same full transform and normalization.
