# Continuous Cantelli rigidity for finite quotients of real power-log functions

Let \(X,Y\) be independent standard normal variables and \(\phi\ge0\) continuous. Suppose that \(\phi^2\) satisfies the finite-quotient germ condition below on each side of every finite point and at both infinities. Then Gaussianity of \(X+\phi(X)Y\) implies that \(\phi\) is constant. In particular, the theorem applies to the continuous class obtained by taking finite quotients of the round 62 algebra, including continuous extensions through their isolated denominator zeros, and to functions pieced together finitely from such quotients. A numerator and a denominator are allowed to depend on the point and side.

The precise germ condition is as follows. For the one-sided variable \(t>0\), use \(x=a\pm t\) at finite \(a\) and \(x=\pm1/t\) at infinity. The germ is a quotient \(N(t,L)/D(t,L)\) evaluated at \(L=\log(1/t)\), with \(D\) nonzero on a sufficiently small punctured interval. Each of \(N,D\) is a finite sum of terms
\[
t^dL^k A(t^{1/q}),\qquad d\in\mathbb R,\quad k\in\mathbb N_0,
\]
where \(q\) is a positive integer and \(A\) is real analytic at zero. This formulation covers germs on sides where an original representation uses different branches. The \(K=\mathbb R\) specialization of Cluckers–Comte–Rolin–Servi's Definition 2.2 is the algebra used as input; the one-variable convergent Puiseux theorem from van den Dries–Miller, §3.1(4) with \(K=\mathbb Q\), gives the displayed generator form. A positive subanalytic generator is \(t^bU(t^{1/q})\) with \(U(0)>0\), so its real power has an analytic unit after factoring out \(t^{b\lambda}\), and its logarithm is \(-bL\) plus an analytic term. [Power-constructible functions](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.04538#S2.SS1) · [Extending Tamm's theorem](https://www.numdam.org/article/AIF_1994__44_5_1367_0.pdf)

## 1. The quotient expansion and ordinary convergence

Choose a common \(q\) and expand each analytic factor. In each of \(N,D\) group the finitely many exponent shifts by congruence modulo \((1/q)\mathbb Z\), and factor out the smallest shift within a group. Each group then has a convergent power series in \(r=t^{1/q}\) with polynomial coefficients in \(L\) of uniformly bounded degree. Factor \(r^j\) from that group up to its first nonzero polynomial coefficient, or omit an identically vanishing group. The supports of the surviving groups are disjoint. In particular, with \(d\) the least exponent of \(D\) and \(D_0\) its nonzero polynomial coefficient, write
\[
D=t^dD_0(L)(1+R(t,L)).
\]
The numerator exponents are in a finite union of translates of \((1/q)\mathbb N_0\). The expansion of \(R\) has strictly positive exponents in a finite union of progressions of that step, and for \(L\) large its absolute majorant is \(O(t^\delta L^B)\) for some \(\delta>0\), \(B\ge0\). This follows from convergence of the finitely many \(r\)-series and the polynomial or reciprocal-polynomial growth of each factor in \(L\). The geometric series for \((1+R)^{-1}\) therefore gives
\[
H(t)=\sum_{e\in E} t^e C_e(L)
\]
with \(C_e\in\mathbb R(L)\). The support \(E\) is bounded below and locally finite: it lies in a finite union of translates of the additive semigroup generated by \(1/q\) and the finitely many strictly positive initial exponents of the progressions for \(R\). Every bounded interval contains only finitely many values from that semigroup since each generator is bounded away from zero. For any fixed \(e\) only finitely many products contribute to \(C_e\).

The expansion is absolute for all sufficiently small \(t\) at \(L=\log(1/t)\). Truncation through an exponent \(e\) has error \(O(t^{e+\eta})\) for a possibly \(e\)-dependent \(\eta>0\). Indeed choose a gap past \(e\), truncate the geometric series at a finite degree so its tail lies past that gap, and truncate each of the finitely many retained analytic series in \(r\). Polynomial factors in \(L\) are absorbed by reducing the gap. The same is true after a fixed number of applications of \(t\partial_t\) to the evaluated expansion: this operator acts as \(t\partial_t-\partial_L\) before specialization, the analytic factors remain analytic, and the geometric tail can be estimated from its finite-power numerator and the bounded inverse \(1/(1+R)\). Every nonzero one-sided germ therefore has the leading form \(ct^pL^k(1+O(1/L))\), \(c\ne0\), \(p\in\mathbb R\), \(k\in\mathbb Z\). In particular a bounded such germ has a finite limit. If \(p=2,k=-m<0\) and \(H>0\), then \(s(t)=\sqrt{H(t)}/t\) satisfies
\[
s(t)^2=cL^{-m}(1+O(1/L)),\qquad
(t\partial_t)^j\log s(t)=O(1/L),\quad j=1,2.
\]
This follows by differentiating the leading rational coefficient \(C_2(L)\), with the higher exponents and their derivatives a fixed positive power smaller. For \(p>2\) the two logarithmic derivatives are bounded.

A separate observation justifies ordinary analytic convergence when all surviving exponents are nonnegative integers and the rational coefficients are constants. Fix \(L_*\in\mathbb R\) with \(D_0(L_*)\ne0\), independently of \(t\). The same geometric expansion is absolutely convergent at \(L_*,t_*>0\) for sufficiently small \(t_*\), since all exponents of \(R\) are positive. Grouping by exponent bounds \(\sum_e|C_e(L_*)|t_*^e\) by that absolute majorant. When the coefficient functions are constants, their specialization changes none of them. The ordinary power series thus has positive radius and equals the original germ by the equally absolute regrouping at \(L=\log(1/t)\). This argument also treats a terminating expansion and a zero germ.

## 2. Compact tilted mass and the two critical zeros

Write \(C=\operatorname{Var}(X+\phi(X)Y)\), \(f=C-\phi^2\), and let \(\gamma\) be standard Gaussian measure. Conditional characteristic functions or real MGFs give
\[
0\le f\le C,\qquad \int f\,d\gamma=1,\qquad
\int e^{tx-t^2f(x)/2}\,\gamma(dx)=1.
\tag{1}
\]
In detail, the conditional real MGF is finite and yields the identity. If \(f\) were negative somewhere, continuity would give a bounded interval of positive mass where \(f\le-\eta<0\), contradicting the identity at large \(t\). The upper bound follows from \(\phi^2\ge0\), and variance gives the mean. The class of quotient germs is closed under adding a constant and negation. We show that for every fixed compact \(K\),
\[
\int_K e^{tx-t^2f(x)/2}\,\gamma(dx)\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{as }|t|\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{2}
\]

A zero interval would make (1) diverge on a subinterval a positive distance from zero under the matching sign of \(t\). Each nonzero germ at a zero thus has positive leading coefficient, with \(p>0\) or \(p=0,k<0\). Consequently all zeros are isolated and there are only finitely many on each compact. The complement of small neighborhoods of these zeros in \(K\) has \(f\) bounded below by a positive number and gives (2).

### A nonzero zero \(a\)

It suffices to take \(T=|t|\) with \(ta=T|a|\), since the other sign gives uniform exponential decay near \(a\) by \(f\ge0\). A positive-order side cannot occur. If \(f(a\pm u)\le A u^{p'}\) for some \(p'>0\), restrict to \(r\le u\le2r\) and take \(T\) a sufficiently small fixed positive multiple of \(r^{-p'}\). Then the exponent in (1) is bounded below there by a fixed positive multiple of \(r^{-p'}\), which beats the length \(r\). For \(p=0,k=-m\) with \(m>1\), make the change \(u=e^{-L}\) and restrict \(L\) to \([T^{2/(m+1)},2T^{2/(m+1)}]\). The Jacobian and variance losses in the exponent are \(O(T^{2/(m+1)})=o(T)\), and \(tx=T|a|+o(1)\). This again contradicts (1).

The only remaining scale on either side is \(f(a\pm u)=c/L+O(L^{-2})\), \(c>0\). Put \(\lambda=\sqrt{c/2}\). At \(L=\lambda T+O(\sqrt T)\) one has
\[
L+\frac{cT^2}{2L}=2\lambda T+\frac{(L-\lambda T)^2}{L}.
\]
The \(O(L^{-2})\) term in \(f\) changes the exponent by \(O(1)\), \(tx=T|a|+o(1)\), and the source density is bounded below by a positive constant on the window. Integration on any such window of length \(\sqrt T\) puts a lower bound \(c'\sqrt T\exp[T(|a|-\sqrt{2c})]\) on the local tilted mass. Thus (1) forces \(c>a^2/2\), including exclusion of equality. To prove decay in this strict case choose \(c_1<c\), \(\eta_1>0\) and \(\theta\in(0,1)\) such that \(|a|+\eta_1<\sqrt{2c_1(1-\theta)}\). Choose the neighborhood small enough that \(f\ge c_1/L\) and \(|x|\le |a|+\eta_1\). Use \((1-\theta)L+c_1T^2/(2L)\ge T\sqrt{2c_1(1-\theta)}\); the remaining factor \(e^{-\theta L}\) is integrable, proving (2) locally.

### A zero at the origin

For each sign of \(t\), only the side of the same sign needs attention, by dominated convergence on the opposite side. Write \(T=|t|\) and \(u=|x|\) on the favorable side. If the leading power \(p<2\), or \(p=2,k\ge0\), then \(f\ge c_1u^2\) for small \(u\). Completion of the square gives the local upper bound \(O(T^{-1})\). If \(p>2\), choose \(p'\in(2,p)\); on \(r\le u\le 2r\), take \(T\) a sufficiently small fixed positive multiple of \(r^{1-p'}\). The exponent has a positive lower bound proportional to \(r^{2-p'}\), and the integral diverges. If \(p=2,k=-m\) with \(m>1\), put \(H=\log T\) and use an interval of \(u=bH^m/T\) with \(b\) in a small fixed interval around \(1/c\), where \(c\) is the leading coefficient. Here \(L=H-m\log H-\log b\) uniformly. The exponent is \(H^m(b-cb^2/2+o(1))\), uniformly positive of that order, which beats the length \(H^m/T\).

It remains to consider
\[
f(\operatorname{sgn}(t)u)=u^2\big(c/L+O(L^{-2})\big),\qquad c>0.
\]
Put \(H=\log T\), \(J=\log H\), and \(b=Tu/H\). On any fixed interval \(\eta\le b\le B\) with \(0<\eta<1/c<B\), the exponent satisfies uniformly
\[
Tu-T^2f/2=bH-\tfrac c2 b^2(H+J)+O(1),
\tag{3}
\]
since \(L=H-J-\log b\) and \(H^2/L=H+J+\log b+O((\log H)^2/H)\). The quadratic in (3) has center \(H/[c(H+J)]\), maximum \(H^2/[2c(H+J)]=(H-J)/(2c)+o(1)\), and width proportional to \(H^{-1/2}\). The Gaussian source density is bounded above and below by positive constants on this interval. Since \(du=H\,db/T\), the integral over \([\eta,B]\) is bounded above and below by positive constants times
\[
T^{1/(2c)-1}H^{1/2-1/(2c)}.
\tag{4}
\]
Thus \(c<1/2\) is impossible. For all \(c\ge1/2\), (4) tends to zero, at equality as \(H^{-1/2}\).

For the remainder choose \(\eta<\min(1,1/c)\) and \(B>\max(1,1/c,4/c_1)\), where \(c_1>0\) is a lower comparison constant \(f\ge c_1u^2/L\) on a fixed small neighborhood \(0<u<r<1\). For \(b\le\eta\) the contribution is at most \(O(H T^{\eta-1})\) by \(f\ge0\). For \(B\le b<rT/H\), \(0<L=H-\log H-\log b\le H\) for large \(T\), so the exponent is at most \(H(b-c_1 b^2/2)\le -c_1Hb^2/4\). Multiplying its integrable bound by \(H/T\) gives a vanishing tail. This establishes (2) on the full neighborhood at the origin.

The tail limits of \(f\) exist by §1. If, for example, the right limit were below 1, apply (1) for \(t\to+\infty\) to the interval \([t-1,t+1]\), after rewriting the integrand with density \(p_1(x-t)\) and factor \(\exp(t^2(1-f(x))/2)\). The integral diverges. If that limit exceeded 1, the same representation bounds the right tail by an exponentially small quantity. The negative tail has at most \(e^{-rt}\) on \(x<-r\), and (2) handles the compact remainder. This also contradicts (1). The left limit equals 1 by reflection. If \(f\) is nonconstant, its mean gives \(M=\max f>1\), attained on a nonempty compact set. Set \(v=M-f\). The full complex transform, or Gaussian characteristic functions, then gives
\[
X+\sqrt{v(X)}\,Z\sim N(0,M),\qquad
\int p_{v(x)+\varepsilon}(y-x)\,\gamma(dx)=p_{M+\varepsilon}(y)
\tag{5}
\]
for an independent standard normal \(Z\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\).

## 3. Local classification of a maximum

At a point \(a\) with \(v(a)=0\), let \(g(u)=p_1(a+u)\). Classify each side as sharp if \(v(a\pm u)/u^2\to\infty\), comparable if that ratio tends to a positive finite number, and flat if it tends to zero or if the side germ is zero. The leading quotient form makes these exhaustive. A comparable side gives a divergent density integral at \(y=a\) in the limit \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) in (5), by Fatou and \(p_{v(a\pm u)}(u)\asymp1/u\). A sharp side likewise can exist only if \(v^{-1/2}\) is integrable on that side, since \(u/\sqrt v\to0\). Its contribution to the unregularized local output is a continuous density, by domination by \(C v^{-1/2}\).

We give the local calculation needed for a flat side. For a positive flat germ on the positive side set \(V(u)=v(a+u)\) for \(0<u<r\), \(s(u)=\sqrt{V(u)}/u\), and \(D_+(y)=\int_0^r g(u)p_{V(u)}(y-u)\,du\), with \(y\) measured from \(a\). The integral \(B_+=\int_0^r g(u)p_{V(u)}(-u)\,du\) is finite. For some \(\kappa>0\),
\[
D_+(y)=B_++{\bf1}_{y>0}I(y)+O(|y|^\kappa),\qquad
I(y)=\int_{y/2}^{2y}g(u)p_{V(u)}(y-u)\,du\quad(y>0).
\tag{6}
\]
For \(|u|\ge |y|^{1/2}\), the derivative bound \( |\partial_z p_\tau(z)|\le C|z|^{-2}\), all \(\tau>0,z\ne0\), gives \(O(|y|^{1/2})\) for the difference after integration. For \(u<|y|^{1/2}\) outside the integral \(I\), one has \(|y-u|\ge u/2\). From the flat germ, \(s(u)^{-1}\exp(-1/(8s(u)^2))\le C u^\delta\) for some \(\delta>0\). Indeed \(s(u)^2\) either tends to zero at a positive power of \(u\), or is \(\asymp L^{-m}\) for an integer \(m\ge1\). Thus this part of the integrand and the corresponding baseline are bounded by \(C u^{\delta-1}\), and (6) follows. For an identically zero germ the source is deterministic and contributes \({\bf1}_{y>0}g(y)\) near zero. Reflection gives the negative-side formulas.

For a positive flat branch make the exact change \(w=y/u-1\) in \(I\):
\[
I(y)=\int_{-1/2}^1 \frac{g(y/(1+w))}{1+w}\,
                   p_{s(y/(1+w))^2}(w)\,dw.
\tag{7}
\]
Write \(s_0=s(y)\), \(q(w)=s(y/(1+w))/s_0\). Bounded logarithmic derivatives of \(s\) give \(q\asymp1\) on this integration interval and \(q(w)=1+O(|w|)\) near zero. The density in (7) is therefore bounded by \(Cs_0^{-1}e^{-c_2(w/s_0)^2}\); replacing \(g(y/(1+w))\) by \(g(0)\) costs \(O(y)\). Scaling \(w=s_0 z\) and using this majorant yields \(I(y)\to g(0)\).

If the flat branch is asymptotically quadratic with inverse logarithm, \(V(u)=u^2cL^{-m}(1+O(1/L))\), \(m\ge1\), (7) gives the more precise
\[
I(y)=g(0)\big(1+c(\log(1/y))^{-m}
                +o((\log(1/y))^{-m})\big).
\tag{8}
\]
In fact on the symmetric interval \(|w|\le1/4\), the two logarithmic derivative bounds of §1 give \(q(w)=1+A(y)w+O(w^2/L_y)\), \(|A(y)|=O(1/L_y)\), \(L_y=\log(1/y)\). The tails in (7) outside that interval are \(O(\exp(-c_3/s_0^2))\), hence \(o(s_0^2)\). Expand \(p_{s_0^2q^2}(w)\) at \(q=1\). For \(q\) in a fixed neighborhood of 1, the first two \(q\)-derivatives are bounded by \(Cs_0^{-1}(1+|w/s_0|^4)e^{-c_4(w/s_0)^2}\). The even first derivative times \(A(y)w\) integrates to zero with unit weight on the symmetric interval. With weight \((1+w)^{-1}\), the cost of replacing this weight by 1 in that term is \(O(s_0^2/L_y)\), by one more factor of \(w\). The \(O(w^2/L_y)\) linear remainder and the quadratic Taylor remainder cost \(O(s_0^2/L_y)\) as well. Finally the constant-\(q\) integral with weight \((1+w)^{-1}\) equals \(1+s_0^2+O(s_0^3)+O(e^{-c_3/s_0^2})\) by symmetry and the first two Gaussian moments. Together with \(O(y)=o(s_0^2)\), these estimates prove (8).

The source outside both local branches gives a Lipschitz output density near \(a\). The indicated derivative bound is uniform in every positive variance for source points a fixed distance away; deterministic exterior contributions have no mass near \(a\). A flat and a sharp side cannot coexist. Equations (6)–(7), or direct evaluation of a deterministic branch, produce a density jump of size \(g(0)\), while the sharp side and exterior are continuous. This contradicts the continuous Gaussian output in (5) even when equality of densities is imposed only almost everywhere.

With two flat sides, suppose one is asymptotically quadratic with inverse logarithm. On that side the opposite flat branch contributes its baseline plus \(O(|y|^\kappa)\), or zero if deterministic. The exterior and baseline terms then give a common limit as \(y\) tends to zero on the chosen side. Equation (8) adds a strictly positive \(g(0)cL_y^{-m}\), while the Gaussian target differs from its limiting value by \(O(|y|)\). The equality almost everywhere is again impossible.

If both flat sides have order strictly above two, counting a zero germ as infinite order, apply the signed residual theorem of round 24 at each first remaining \(t\)-exponent. Start with the reference polynomial \(P=0\) in the signed coordinate \(u=x-a\). The exponent is \(\alpha>2\) and at least one side has a nonzero rational coefficient. At this exponent choose the highest leading logarithm power among the two surviving coefficients. The residual theorem forces this power to be zero, \(\alpha\) to be an integer, and the leading constants to match with the signed parity of \(\alpha\). In particular they are both nonzero. Subtract the corresponding ordinary monomial. Any surviving coefficient at the same exponent is rational with zero limit at \(L=\infty\) and leads with a strictly negative integer logarithm power, which the residual theorem excludes. Thus the entire coefficient has been removed. The first matched ordinary monomial has positive coefficient and even degree at least four, by \(v\ge0\) on both sides. All later partial polynomials are therefore nonnegative in a neighborhood and \(O(u^4)\), as required by round 24. Local finiteness of the union of exponent sets permits induction. By the specialization argument in §1 the resulting common power series genuinely converges. If at least one side is a nonzero germ, this contradicts the analytic maximum theorem of round 23.

If both sides are sharp, round 17 gives finiteness of every local negative moment of \(v\). Consequently neither side can have a positive \(t\)-power, regardless of its logarithm factor. Since \(v(a)=0\), it follows that each side is a pure inverse logarithm,
\[
v(a\pm u)\asymp (\log(1/u))^{-m_\pm},\qquad m_\pm\in\mathbb N.
\tag{9}
\]
The arguments thus leave only two possibilities at any maximum: the point is an interior point of the maximum set, or it is isolated and has (9) on both sides. If the maximum set had a nonempty interior, any finite boundary point of one of its interval components would have a deterministic flat side and a nonzero side by the germ condition. All such cases were excluded above. Hence every maximum is isolated. Compactness of the maximum set makes their number finite.

## 4. Simultaneous Fourier bound with distinct logarithm powers

Let the maxima be \(a_1,\dots,a_J\). Choose disjoint intervals of small radii \(r_j<e^{-1}\) around them where (9) holds with exponents \(m_{j,+}\) and \(m_{j,-}\), independently. Off these intervals there is a positive lower bound for \(v\), by continuity and the tail limits \(f\to1<M\). Write \(m_*=\max_{j,\pm}m_{j,\pm}\). We spell out round 18's extension to this asymmetric setting.

There are arbitrarily large positive integers \(q\) for which every \(q(a_j-a_1)\) is within \(1/4\) of \(2\pi\mathbb Z\). Apply the pigeonhole principle to the first \(Q^{J-1}+1\) integer multiples of the vector of differences divided by \(2\pi\), partitioning the torus into \(Q^{J-1}\) cubes. If the resulting integer denominators are bounded along a subsequence, one is an exact common denominator and its multiples suffice. At these \(q\), rotate the characteristic identity from (5) by \(e^{-iq a_1}\). The real part of the phase factor is at least \(\cos(1/2)>1/2\) whenever \(|x-a_j|\le1/(4q)\). Off this union the lower bounds in (9), together with the positive exterior gap, give \(v(x)\ge c/(\log(4q))^{m_*}\). The total absolute contribution off the union is at most \(\exp[-cq^2/(2(\log(4q))^{m_*})]\).

Choose only one specified side of one maximum, with exponent \(m>0\). On \(e^{-L}\le |x-a_j|\le2e^{-L}\) there, take \(L=q^{2/(m+1)}\). This interval lies in the phase-positive region for large \(q\). Its length and the upper bound \(v\le C/L^m\) give a rotated real contribution at least \(c'\exp[-C'q^{2/(m+1)}]\). The exponent power is below 2, so that contribution dominates both the possible exterior loss and the exact target \(e^{-Mq^2/2}\). This contradicts (5) and completes the proof.

## Previous local results used

Round 17 is used only with a two-sided sharp contact and the regularized identity (5). Round 24 is used only after establishing order strictly above two, at each step with a nonnegative local polynomial reference of order at least four or the zero polynomial. Round 23 is used only after proving analytic convergence and checking that the analytic germ at the attained maximum is nonzero. The Fourier argument in §4 allows independent left and right inverse logarithm powers, even at one maximum. The general continuous case continues to allow other germ structures.
