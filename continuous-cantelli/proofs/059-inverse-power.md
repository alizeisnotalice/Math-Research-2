# A supremum approached along sectorially regular power-law tails

Assume the normalized variance v=M−f is continuous bounded, v(x)>0 at every finite x and inf v=0. The Gaussian transform identity is
∫ e^{iξx−ξ²v(x)/2}γ(dx)=e^{−Mξ²/2}, M>0.
On at least one end require a positive power tail, meaning that for some d,p>0 the variance on x=±r, r large, extends as an analytic function V(r) into an exterior sector containing 0≤arg r≤θ+ε with θ=π/(2(p+1)), ε>0, and V(z)/(d z^−p)→1 uniformly for 0≤arg z≤θ+ε (a smaller margin suffices). The power uses the branch real on (0,∞). On the other end assume either another such tail, with its own parameters, or a strict positive variance lower bound. These hypotheses contradict exactness. No local smoothness, lower bound for f, or regularity near any zero of f is required. In particular arbitrary continuous compact gluing of exact inverse power tails is included.

For a single positive power tail write a=(pdξ/2)^{1/(p+1)}, λ=ξa and Δ=a/ξ→0. Ignoring the fixed Gaussian normalization, the tail integral on x≥R, R large and fixed, is
a ∫_{R/a}^∞ exp[λ F_ξ(z)]dz,
F_ξ(z)=iz−(ξ/(2a))V(az)−Δ z²/2.
On compact annular sets interior to the sector it converges with its first three derivatives to F(z)=iz−z^−p/p. At z₀=e^{iθ} the latter has a simple stationary point. Along z=r e^{iθ},
F=i e^{iθ}(r+r^{-p}/p).
Its real part has its unique maximum at r=1, equal to −A, A=(1+1/p)sinθ>0. Its imaginary part there is B=(1+1/p)cosθ>0, and the second derivative along the ray is (p+1)i e^{iθ}, with negative real part.

The following deformation accounts for small and large radii and for p<1. Choose φ with 0<φ<min(θ,π/4). Move the initial endpoint R up on the arc |x|=R from the real ray to angle θ; choose R so large that Re V(x)≥c R^−p on this arc since cos(pθ)>0. Its contribution is O(exp[−c'ξ²]); the imaginary-frequency part supplies no growth there. Follow the ray θ until z=L e^{iθ} with large fixed L, then bend back to φ along |z|=L and continue along this ray out to infinity. This is a legitimate deformation; the arcs at infinity for angles 0…φ are controlled by the Gaussian source since cos(2φ)>0. For R/a≤r≤δ small along θ the bound Re F_ξ≤−c r^−p +O(Δ δ²) holds uniformly, enough for a fixed gap below −A. On the bend, Re F_ξ≤−L sinφ+O(Δ L²), and afterwards Re F_ξ≤−r sinφ since Re V>0 there and cos(2φ)>0. Thus choosing δ small and L large leaves only a compact annular contour relevant. On its parts apart from the saddle neighborhood local uniform convergence gives a strict exponential gap.

The simple zero of F_ξ' near z₀ exists uniquely for all large ξ by Rouché; denote it z_ξ. It is continuous in ξ and tends to z₀. Translate a short segment of the θ-ray near the saddle to pass through z_ξ, keeping direction e^{iθ}; short connectors can be placed in the fixed gap. On this segment the Gaussian saddle estimate by r−r_* scaled by √λ gives
I_+(ξ)=a λ^−1/2 exp[λ S(ξ)] (C(ξ)+o(1)),
where S=F_ξ(z_ξ)→−A+iB and C(ξ)→C_∞=e^{iθ}√(2π)/√[−(p+1)i e^{iθ}] times the omitted source normalization. The square root is the continuous one with positive real part. All statements follow from the uniformly negative real second derivative, bounded third derivative on the local contour and Gaussian dominated convergence. In particular log|I_+(ξ)|/λ→−A. A left power tail contributes the complex conjugate of the same positive-ray calculation for its real-input profile.

The compact complement contributes at most exp(−c ξ²) since v>0 there; the same bound holds for a uniformly positive opposite tail. Since λ scales as ξ^{(p+2)/(p+1)}=o(ξ²), a single power tail cannot match the desired Gaussian transform. With two power tails of different p the one with larger p has the smaller exponent power and dominates in magnitude. With equal p but unequal d the smaller d gives the smaller multiple of ξ^{(p+2)/(p+1)} in the real action and dominates. If both parameters agree, use the common a,λ. The difference in arguments of the two leading terms (right and conjugated left) is a continuous function equal to (2B+o(1))λ, permitting a sequence ξ→∞ where it is a multiple of 2π. Indeed choose continuous arguments of the C(ξ), and use the intermediate value theorem; monotonicity is unnecessary. The leading terms then add by magnitude regardless of possible smaller-order differences in their real actions, and the individual relative o(1) errors cannot cancel them. This finishes the contradiction.
