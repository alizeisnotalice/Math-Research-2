# Sharp cost of a common Lipschitz bound in approximation of the mixed law

Fix the ε=1/8 joint target μ of round 145. Use the metric |x−x'|+|F−F'| on joint laws and its first Wasserstein distance. For large L the infimum of this distance over deterministic globally L-Lipschitz graph laws with the standard Gaussian spatial marginal is bounded above and below by positive constants times L^(−1/2). The lower bound already uses the graph's modulus on a single fixed bounded interval I of positive length. This quantifies the failure of a common modulus when approximating this particular relaxed law. Exactness of the transform by itself does not imply that a graph approximates this law in joint Wasserstein distance.

**Lower bound.** Choose I=[−1,0]. On I the middle and late target layers, of heights 1 and 1+ε, each have Lebesgue density bounded below by some p>0: the late density is a strictly positive continuous Gaussian convolution, and the middle has a uniform lower bound by round 128. The target puts zero mass at all heights in the open gap (1,1+ε), everywhere on the line. Write D for the distance to a given continuous graph graph(g), understood as the Wasserstein distance between laws, and first assume D small. Partition I into N equal closed cells of length h comparable to K D, with K a sufficiently large fixed constant (take N=floor(1/(KD))). Call a cell good if it contains one graph height within ε/8 of each of the two levels. If a cell misses one level, the corresponding target mass in the middle half of that cell costs at least min(h/4,ε/8) per unit in every coupling: any graph point outside the cell moves spatially by at least h/4, and an inside point moves vertically by at least ε/8. For small D each bad cell therefore costs at least p h²/8. Summing disjoint target portions shows at least N/2 cells are good if K>16/p.

By continuity each good cell contains a subinterval traversing the heights 1+ε/4 and 1+3ε/4 and staying between them: take the last departure from the first level before the first arrival at the second, reversing the spatial orientation if needed. Under a Lipschitz bound L that subinterval has length at least ε/(2L). All its graph mass must move vertically by at least ε/4 under any coupling with μ, including couplings that move it outside I. Since the Gaussian density has a strictly positive minimum on I, summing over good cells gives D≥c/(Lh), hence D≥c'/√L. If D is not small this same bound holds for large L by reducing the constant. More generally for any common modulus ω on I the same proof gives D≥c√r for each sufficiently small r with ω(r)<ε/2. Together with h comparable to D this follows because each crossing subinterval has length at least r. The positive constants here depend on I and the fixed target.

**Matching upper bound.** Divide the entire real line into cells of length h=L^(−1/2). On each cell subdivide into at most three intervals carrying the correct three Gaussian layer masses, and assign the associated constant heights. A coupling matches each layer separately inside its cell at spatial cost at most h. Smooth this piecewise constant profile by convolution with a fixed smooth probability kernel supported on [−r,r]. Its range stays in [1−ε,1+ε] and its Lipschitz constant is at most 2ε times the L¹ norm of the derivative of the unit kernel divided by r. Choose r a sufficiently large fixed multiple of 1/L so the required Lipschitz bound holds. The modification only affects points within r of a jump, and at most a fixed number of such strips intersect each cell for r<h/3. Their total Gaussian mass is at most C r/h, since h times the sum of the suprema of the Gaussian density on the cells is bounded uniformly for h≤1 (monotonicity on the two wings suffices). Coupling before and after smoothing at identical x costs ≤C r/h. Together these give D≤C/√L. This upper construction is for the quantitative joint-law approximation. The exact growing finite calibration and uniform real-transform error are addressed by round 145 without a Lipschitz rate.
